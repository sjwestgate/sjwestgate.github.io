So, if you've read Bubble Thoughts, you'll know that I consider myself an experimentalist and was hoping to continue in experimental physics for my fourth-year MPhys project.
Alas, the lab-partner-finding-event and the optimization algorithm were not in my favour.

I wanted to do an experimental project outside of fluids/biophysics/soft matter physics. Unfortunately that narrowed down my options massively and to find a lab partner who was happy with this specific subset of projects proved too difficult, so in the end I decided to widen my horizons a bit. I still wanted to try something new, though, and that then meant writing down theoretical projects.
I have had my own battle with the concept of theoretical physics for a while. I take a lot of theory-heavy courses, for example Gauge Theories at the moment, and QFT and GR last semester. I tend to enjoy them a lot as well, it's more fun for me to do a big maths problem and come out with a solution than it is to write about applications of whatever or how you do an experiment. I think that this also is why I'm interested in experimental research and not in our undergraduate labs; doing research is a big problem to solve as opposed to the first year 'here's an experiment everyone else has done -> do it the same way they did -> get the same results'. In my head I think I was hoping I'd be a theorist just to say that I was a theorist, if that makes sense, I wanted to be impressive like that. Of course experimental physics is still impressive and has the advantage that I enjoy doing it that bit more; I love being in the lab and playing with lasers and vacuums and such (more on that later). But eventually I decided that theory is just not for me. 

The thing about this process was that it didn't actually involve me doing any theoretical physics research and so I couldn't visualise the life of a theorist, surely they couldn't just write down equations all day? So why not write one down, if we end up on the project I can give it a go and see if I'm secretly quite good at it?

And of course we ended up on the theory project "Reaction co-ordinate mappings in fermionic environments". Don't worry, I didn't know what all that meant either. In fact it was quite an interesting project. It falls within the realm of open quantum systems research, where instead of the nice, non-interacting picture you so often have in undergrad, you actually take the environment into account. In practice, this involves writing down an equation for the so-called density matrix of the system (which is sort of a more complicated wavefunction), and then making some approximations. You say that maybe the environment doesn't evolve while the system does, and the system doesn't oscillate quickly or remember its previous states. And then you get a nice-looking thing that you can put into QuTiP and solve for all sorts of different observables.
The reaction co-ordinate mapping itself involves taking a collective degree of freedom from the environment that you then treat as part of the system, so you sort of treat a bit of the environment exactly, and then make the same approximations on the rest of the environment. We found that it worked really well, actually, when we could get the code to work. Of course the process of building up to this point actually took the whole project so we didn't find anything new, but it was still a massively insightful process. 

In the end I was really happy I did it. I got so bored of sitting in the university library and battling with Python and writing down equations, but I'm glad I gave myself the chance to give it a go instead of just writing it off as too boring/difficult. It also helped a bit with my PhD applications: I feel that I can paint myself as quite a well-rounded applicant who has a passion for experiment while also having a strong background in theory. Open quantum systems also helps with the specific niche that I'm going into, so it's cool to have that different perspective on it.
